Introducing ExtractBenchPro: the most challenging benchmark for real-world document extraction. We see a massive long tail of document complexity in terms of use cases brought on from our customers, that are completely unsolved from a price-performance standpoint. These include trial balances, well logs, canvass binders, site plan reviews, duty reconciliations, loss runs, and more. Most existing extraction benchmarks are saturated by the frontier models (our cheapest modes now score 93%+ on ExtractBench). So there is enormous economic value in building a complex extraction benchmark that reflects this long-tail of knowledge work. We spent a long time curating the most complex elements that we saw from real world extraction use cases: ✅ Document conventions: containing legends or rules where the rest of the document should be interpreted ✅ Density of internal jargon: specific definitions that must be cross-referenced within a doc ✅ Aggregation and reasoning: beyond returning exact values on a page, infer values derived from aggregating multiple sources of information ✅ Drawn relations: complex diagrams like procedures and circuits ✅ Charts inside documents: read precise points within a chart ✅ Long chart sheets: some data series like well logs cram all this data into an extremely long page Within the industries where document extraction is relevant, even small mistakes can have enormous negative impacts. Therefore our headline evaluation metric is based on perfect documents: the proportion of docs with perfect (100%) value f1 score. (Besides this we also measure value f1 and grounded f1) It’s an extremely hard task for even the latest frontier models to solve, topping out at ~58% or so in Value F1 score for Codex with Astra - which is quite good but extremely expensive (almost $1 per page!) Our LlamaParse agentic/agentic plus modes are 20-40% and cover the left side of the Pareto frontier, at price ranges that are feasible for full production use. ExtractBenchPro is available today as a website/technical blog, and also on Github and HuggingFace. Check it out! Terminal Agent 很容易遇到一种失败是前面修对了 90%，最后一个 hidden test 没过，最终结果还是失败。 这篇 Terminal-RSI 研究的，就是怎么把这种「差一点成功」也变成下一轮真正有用的训练信号。 它交替做两件事。 RSR 会从已经验证成功的轨迹里提炼可复现的做法，再教回 Agent； RPR 则继续用强化学习消化执行反馈，让局部进展可以获得 credit，同时保证「真正完成任务」始终比只通过部分检查更重要。 论文还有一个很细的设计是 Terminal 里一次命令可能吐出几千 token 日志，但期间 Agent 根本没有做新决策。 所以它把信用分配的时间尺度从「token 数量」改到「交互轮次」，避免日志越长，前面的关键决策反而被稀释。 基于 Qwen3.8-27B，经过两轮 RSR + RPR 后，四个 Terminal-Bench 的平均成功率从 20.68% 提升到 50.58%；Terminal-Bench Hard 从 31% 提升到 79%。 我比较喜欢这篇对「失败轨迹」的处理方式。 一次任务没通过终验，不代表前面所有判断都没有价值。 真正好的自我改进系统，应该能把已经走对的部分留下来，同时知道最后为什么还差那一步。 📎 arxiv: https://t.co/mPakaVb7Sg

Some brief takeaways: - There's an increasing understanding that greater liquidity in compute markets requires the construction of unifying benchmark indices, so that bespoke/OTC markets can be priced/quoted as a diff to a shared set of benchmarks, similar to what exists in the energy complex already (think crude grades and power). Part of the discussion is whether this will organically emerge if more traditional players (Citadel, Jump, blue-chip banks) expand their OTC offering for bilateral hedges (using their own prices), or require an independent party (S&P, CME, etc.) to validate and give credence for broader adoption. - Massive growth in the compute space is largely underpinned by structured financing trades with hedging mandates (similar to what you see in the oil and gas space). If someone with a sizable balance sheet just lent you a lot of money, who's likely to give you more aggressive pricing than their sales and trading arm? - Funding from life insurers has been a key part of the data center buildout (you could call it duration matching), with an increasing number of insurers considering hedging compute. Some of this exposure sits in mortgage books, while the biggest bucket is via corporate bonds ($ORCL, $MSFT, $META, and $GOOG comprise nearly 80% of Athene's IG software debt). - Quite a few folks seem offered over on the backend of the curve, largely driven by: * Concerns that distressed sellers could flood the market if the AI growth story slows down (as there's a lot of GPU-backed debt to unwind) * Natural hedgers are mostly sellers, as operators need contracted revenue to borrow, so they sell further out on the curve; buyers mostly lock in near-term rates w/ marginal demand coming from a handful of labs whose spending is contingent on their ability to still tap into financing * Software improvements are reducing GPU-hour needs, all the while new chips are depreciating the value of old hardware per unit of compute: $NVDA is releasing a new gen roughly every year (Blackwell Ultra, Rubin, and Rubin Ultra). That said, it does look like old chips are staying useful longer than most depreciation models assume, since inference and smaller models still allow sufficient monetization of older hardware.
