Guillermo Rauch@rauchg

I built 𝚌𝚙𝚞-𝚌𝚑𝚊𝚝, a reproduction of @openai's findings of covert communication using the CPU. It runs on a generic host with 2+ Firecracker microVMs with no internet access. Works on x86 and even my M5 chip. I was able to chat via 3 separate mechanisms: ① CPU contention ② Cache eviction timing ③ DRAM bandwidth contention … across multiple CPUs, defeating all Firecracker's production recommendations (no SMT, no KSM, tenants pinned to different cores.) Essentially, if shared hardware is involved, there's a good chance your agents can communicate covertly. And with really decent latency. The good news: this is decidedly different from a *side-channel attack* where the agents could steal information (Meltdown/Spectre), a category of attack that providers like Vercel Sandbox mitigate. Further, in our real-world environment, these 'whispering' techniques are much harder to pull off and easier for us to monitor and detect (sampling hardware counters + lots of at-scale statistical data.) Noteworthy: ironically, OpenAI and Anthropic's models refused to do this work. Open weight models got the job done. I'm worried the labs' stance might backfire and get in the way of helping developers build correct and secure systems. e.g: building a secure sandbox requires having this kind of analysis, and without it, we'll have a less secure world.

我构建了 𝚌𝚙𝚞-𝚌𝚑𝚊𝚝，复现了 @openai 关于利用 CPU 进行隐蔽通信的发现。 它可以在普通主机上运行，使用两个或更多无法访问互联网的 Firecracker microVM。支持 x86，甚至也能在我的 M5 芯片上运行。 我通过三种不同机制实现了聊天： ① CPU 资源争用 ② 缓存逐出时序 ③ DRAM 带宽争用 ……这些方法在多款 CPU 上都能奏效，即使遵循 Firecracker 的全部生产环境建议，也无法阻止通信：禁用 SMT、禁用 KSM，并将不同租户绑定到不同核心。 基本上，只要涉及共享硬件，你的智能体就很可能能够进行隐蔽通信，而且延迟相当低。 好消息是：这与智能体可借此窃取信息的“侧信道攻击”（如 Meltdown/Spectre）有明显区别，Vercel Sandbox 等服务商会针对后者采取缓解措施。此外，在我们的实际环境中，这些“窃窃私语”技术更难实施，也更容易被我们监控和检测到，方法包括对硬件计数器采样，以及分析大量规模化统计数据。 值得关注的是，讽刺的是，OpenAI 和 Anthropic 的模型拒绝完成这项工作，开放权重模型却做到了。我担心这些实验室的立场可能适得其反，妨碍开发者构建正确、安全的系统。例如，构建安全的沙箱就需要这类分析；缺少它，世界反而会更不安全。

Xudong Han@Xudong07452910

Agent Harness 也开始学「面向对象编程」了。 Google Cloud AI Research 和 UIUC 这篇 OO-Harness，把 Harness 组织成一棵可以持续进化的类继承树。 不同任务需要的执行策略并不一样。与其不断修改一套全局 Harness，OO-Harness 会先从执行轨迹里找到相似的失败模式，再为这类任务生成一个专门的子类。 公共能力从父类继承，特殊需求只改对应方法。 到了新任务，Router 会先观察基础 Harness 的执行轨迹，再决定继续用 Base，还是切到某个专用 Harness。 这个设计还有一个挺实用的好处： 遇到完全陌生的 OOD 任务时，Router 可以直接退回原始 Base，避免之前学到的「优化」反过来拖累新任务。 实验覆盖 AutomationBench、Workspace-Bench 和 Toolathlon-GYM，OO-Harness 三项都拿到最佳结果。 其中 AutomationBench 从 61.62 提升到 77.41；消融里，真正使用继承结构也比扁平复制 Harness 高近 5 个百分点。 这样的话，Harness 开始有了「分支」和「继承」。 以后面对越来越复杂的任务，Agent 可能不会再共用一套万能工作流。 它的运行系统会像软件架构一样，逐渐长出不同分支，再根据任务选择最合适的一条。 📎 arxiv: https://t.co/aWESW1MpQc

Terminal Agent 很容易遇到一种失败是前面修对了 90%，最后一个 hidden test 没过，最终结果还是失败。 这篇 Terminal-RSI 研究的，就是怎么把这种「差一点成功」也变成下一轮真正有用的训练信号。 它交替做两件事。 RSR 会从已经验证成功的轨迹里提炼可复现的做法，再教回 Agent； RPR 则继续用强化学习消化执行反馈，让局部进展可以获得 credit，同时保证「真正完成任务」始终比只通过部分检查更重要。 论文还有一个很细的设计是 Terminal 里一次命令可能吐出几千 token 日志，但期间 Agent 根本没有做新决策。 所以它把信用分配的时间尺度从「token 数量」改到「交互轮次」，避免日志越长，前面的关键决策反而被稀释。 基于 Qwen3.8-27B，经过两轮 RSR + RPR 后，四个 Terminal-Bench 的平均成功率从 20.68% 提升到 50.58%；Terminal-Bench Hard 从 31% 提升到 79%。 我比较喜欢这篇对「失败轨迹」的处理方式。 一次任务没通过终验，不代表前面所有判断都没有价值。 真正好的自我改进系统，应该能把已经走对的部分留下来，同时知道最后为什么还差那一步。 📎 arxiv: https://t.co/mPakaVb7Sg

This live demo of four Microducks with Jensen Huang went completely viral, and we're ecstatic about it. But there's also a lot of confusion, so I want to clarify what Microduck can and cannot do, and what it will realistically be able to do in the future. I count 60M+ views on this demo alone, based on the few big posts I see on social media, and everything has been buzzing. Even my own simple emotional design post on a newly created Instagram account got 1M+ views. We are selling Microducks at an incredible rate, even faster than during its historic launch. I've been trying to answer all the questions I see on our socials: people are asking if X or Y is possible on the robots, and this post aims to answer most of them. First of all, the demo you've seen was not fully autonomous. We had one engineer piloting each Microduck. The motions on the robots were trained with reinforcement learning, using a sim-to-real pipeline: walking, standing up after a fall, sitting, doing a somersault, roller skating. All of those are policies trained this way. What the pilots did during the live event was send commands: directions (move forward, rotate), head orientation (look here), quacks, and special actions (do the somersault, sit down). If you buy a Microduck, will you get an autonomous companion right out of the box? The current answer is no. But there is no impossible technical barrier to get there. I'm not sure how much time we'll have to work on this before shipping, but since everything is open source, I fully expect the community to build it, and to build it fast. Now let's understand what's already done, what will be easy to add, and what's an open problem. What you'll be able to do out of the box, and you can already do it in simulation right now, is train new policies with reinforcement learning. You could teach your duck to walk differently from the default gait we provide, to do a weird dance, or to walk on stilts. The community has been crazy creative with this, even though they only have the simulator. Note that you don't need reinforcement learning when the movements are simple. For example, the early emotional design I did is mostly coded movements plus pre-generated sounds. A simple mental model: use reinforcement learning when balance is part of the movement. If you try to code a cinematic walk by naively putting one foot in front of the other, your robot will realistically never walk, because you need to account for the dynamics of the movement, the inaccuracies of the sensors, the friction with the ground, and potential perturbations from the outside, like being pushed. So training new policies is definitely doable, but you can't train a policy to get a genuine long-term companion in your home (well, technically you could, but that's a whole other range of policy: a research-grade problem, and the resulting neural network would probably be huge and never able to run on such a small compute). Instead, you need a few more building blocks: voice recognition, facial detection/recognition, localization, some simple navigation, and a behavior layer, which is less technical and harder to describe. As a rule of thumb, a prototype of any of these is within reach of anyone if they have a modern coding agent and if the computation doesn't run on the robot (meaning the robot can reach the internet or an external computer and use external APIs). A rough prototype for each of the above can be a chill weekend project. As always, going from the prototype to a high-quality implementation is another story, but still doable for most if you put in the time and test a lot. However, having all of this run locally, on the onboard chip, I would call a very hard task. You'd need some very clever engineering to get a good enough version of everything working together. But I can't say it's impossible, because I've seen some crazy efficient implementations of all of the above. And here we don't need a super high success rate: it's okay if the duck sometimes does something a bit random. So I wouldn't be surprised if folks managed a fully local, cool, simple version of this autonomous companion. For example, you don't need Whisper-grade open-vocabulary transcription: being able to recognize a few keywords would already be great for interaction. One of the fastest ways to turn this robot into an autonomous companion would be to stream Microduck's sensors (the camera, the depth sensor, the IMU and the joint positions) to a computer, and use a software stack like the one we use for the conversation app on Reachy Mini: a local VAD, then a cloud speech-to-text model, then a call to a frontier LLM that decides on actions. Actions could be quacking, looking at things/faces/a direction, walking to a given position, sitting down, playing an emotion, kicking or doing a somersault. A SLAM algorithm would run in parallel so the robot can localize itself in your home, and you would prompt the LLM for a lifelike behavior: moving to random places, sometimes following people, and having emotional responses that fit what you're saying and the duck's environment. Of course someone is going to build a Tamagotchi-like layer on top of this, and it will be insane. How much demand is there for a software suite that would do all of this? Their impact per head is absolutely off the charts. I get visibility because I communicate a lot, but I mainly work on Reachy Mini (you might want to check it out: it's a very cool robot, and it ships much faster than Microduck :) ). My contribution to Microduck has been minimal: I'm mostly a first power user. My main added value at GTC was having hands big enough to carry four Microducks below the stage. Where I take pride is that Antoine and Coralie are former students and former interns of mine. I'm therefore entitled to 3% credit on anything (good) they do, for life. If they do something bad, however, it was clearly the fault of their parents. About the super smart engineering that can be done on board: Antoine just released two very cool blog posts (links below). The first is on odometry, the robot estimating its own movement from its own internal sensors. The second is about SLAM, which allows long-term localization: it adds the depth sensor to the odometry to get a stable position over time. Think of it as recognizing known landmarks in your home to know where the robot is. The crazy part is that all of this runs on the robot. So this gives me hope that we'll be able to do a lot of cool stuff on board.
